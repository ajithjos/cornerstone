# Written Methods Teaching Note

This adult guide holds the teaching sequence, correction cues, and answer keys. Give the learner the lesson and the selected practice level; keep this guide for marking.

## Start from readiness, not age

For a seven-year-old, first check that he or she can name tens and ones, build `34` as three tens and four ones, and exchange ten ones for one ten. Begin with two digits and one digit without exchange. Add one new demand at a time.

For a nine-year-old, use a short baseline: `34 + 5`, `55 + 68`, `83 - 27`, and `503 - 267`. Ask the learner to show the working and explain one carry or exchange. Begin at the first insecure level. A nine- or ten-year-old with secure addition and subtraction can start short multiplication; age alone does not establish readiness for long division.

Keep basic addition/subtraction facts and multiplication tables in short daily review. Support a weak fact with a known fact or a reference table during teaching; assess independently only after the needed facts and method have been taught.

## A useful 12 to 18 minute session

1. Retrieve two earlier facts or calculations.
2. Teach one new step in two or three minutes. Model it on squared paper while naming the places.
3. Work one example together. Ask the learner to write the next step.
4. Give four to six independent questions of that type. Shorten a set of long calculations if careful work needs more time.
5. Mark promptly. Explain the first incorrect step, work one similar example, then ask for a fresh independent attempt.
6. Finish with a question from an earlier level.

A full worksheet is a bank of levels, not a single sitting. More pages are useful only when mistakes are corrected. Stop when attention is no longer sufficient for accurate practice.

## Progression within each operation

| Operation | First foundation | Next steps | Extension |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Addition | Two digits plus one, no carry | Two plus two; one carry; three plus one/two; repeated carries | More columns with the same rule |
| Subtraction | Two digits minus one, no exchange | Two minus two; one exchange; three-digit exchange; exchange across zero | Several zeros and larger numbers |
| Multiplication | Two digits by one, no carry | Carrying; three digits by one; two by two; three by two | More multiplier places; zero multiplier digits |
| Division | Two digits by one, exact with no exchange | Exchanges; remainders; three digits; zero quotient places; two-digit divisors | Longer dividends and remainders |

These are separate skills. A child may practise addition at one level and subtraction at an earlier level. Introduce short division after multiplication facts and equal grouping are understood; do not require mastery of every long-multiplication extension first.

For an informal decision to move up one practice level, look for at least five correct answers out of six on two separate short independent sets, including the new pattern, and a correct explanation of one carry, exchange, partial product, or remainder. Repeated errors in the new pattern mean more work at that level even if the total score is high. This is adult teaching guidance; it is not an automatic app mastery or confirmation rule.

## Assisted practice and independent assessment

In assisted practice, explain the first mistaken step immediately. Use a hint about its place value before showing a complete worked answer. After a worked answer, give a fresh question so copying is not counted as independent success.

In an independent assessment, allow no hints or worked answers until submission. Mark the attempt, then discuss and correct each error. A corrected answer after help is valuable practice, but does not replace the original independent result. Do not make this a speed contest; fluent, accurate work comes first.

Printed practice and on-screen practice should use the same written structure. On paper, leave a row for carries and plenty of room for each partial product or long-division subtraction. A marked final answer alone cannot show whether the method was correct; inspect the working too.

## Marking subtraction exchanges

Compare **987 − 128** with **987 − 198**. In both, the tens give one away, so the child writes **7** above the 8. Ask “How many tens will you subtract from?” In the first example, seven tens are enough. In the second, the child trades a hundred for ten more tens. Say “Keep the 7 and think seventeen.” Accept either **7** or **17** in that working box. Check that the child uses 17 − 9 for the tens answer.

## Correction cues

- Misaligned numbers: “Point to the ones in each number. Put those in one column.”
- Omitted carry: “Those thirteen ones are one ten and three ones. Where must the ten go?”
- Carry multiplied again: “Multiply the original digit, then add the amount carried.”
- Subtraction reversed: “There are only three ones. Exchange one ten before subtracting seven ones.”
- Exchange across zero: “Find the next place that has something to exchange; trade through each place.”
- Unshifted multiplication row: “This digit means twenty. Twenty groups are ten times two groups.”
- Missing quotient zero: “There are zero whole tens in each share. Keep the tens place.”
- Excessive remainder: “Can you make another complete group of the divisor?”

Use `carry` or `borrow` if those match school language, while insisting on what was exchanged. In subtraction nothing is paid back: the number has been renamed without changing its value.
